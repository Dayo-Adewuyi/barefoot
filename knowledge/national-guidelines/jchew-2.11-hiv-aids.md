2.11. HIV/AIDS
HIV/AIDS is caused by a virus, which infects human beings. AIDS is the disease condition resulting from low
immunity. Babies born to HIV-infected women may become infected during pregnancy, delivery or through
breastfeeding. At the same time, many babies who are not breastfed because of fear of HIV transmission may
die from diarrhoea, pneumonia or other diseases (not related to HIV) because they are not protected by
immune substances found in breast milk.
Thus, HIV–infected mothers need to be well guided on how best to feed their babies to ensure that more
babies survive without becoming infected with HIV. Women can be given Antiretrovirals to reduce the
chances of transmitting the virus to their babies. Babies born to HIV positive women are referred to as HIV
exposed babies, a baby can only be said to be HIV positive after diagnostic confirmation.

    Infant Feeding in the Context of HIV:
    Malnutrition is a common condition in HIV infected children and a major contributor to mortality in this
    population. HIV infection can result in nutritional deficiencies, growth failure and development delay.
    Malnutrition itself in decreased immunity and greater susceptibility to infections.Thus accelerating disease
    progression. Malnutrition makes HIV infection worse and HIV infection worsens malnutrition.


    Health Education
    Advise mother/caregiver on:

    1.Factors known to increase the risk of mother –to-child transmission and prevention.
    2.The continuous use of ART (Nevirapine to babies)
    3.Adequate nutrition.
    4.Immunization.
    5.Periodical deworming.
    6.Regular growth monitoring.
    7.Use of Long-Lasting Insecticide Nets (LLINs).
    8.Personal, food and environmental hygiene.
    9.The importance of attending ante-natal care (ANC) in order to provide appropriate care for the mother
    and foetus.
    10.Importance of taking home-based records on child’s next visit to the clinic or hospital.

2.12. MEASLES – TYPE RASH
Measles is an acute viral infection characterized by the appearance of rashes in small groups consisting of
macules (flat spots) and papules (raised spots). It typically begins with high fever, cough, nasal congestion,
redness of the eyes, and inflammation of the mucous membranes of the mouth. Later stages may include
mouth sores, followed by the appearance of rashes across the entire body after approximately the fourth
day of illness onset.
In addition to the characteristic rash, children with measles may also experience other serious complications
such as pneumonia, diarrhea, or malnutrition, either concurrently or shortly after the onset of the disease.
Measles can be particularly severe in poorly nourished children and may lead to various complications,
underscoring the importance of timely intervention and appropriate medical care.
